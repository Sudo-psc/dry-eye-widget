Presenteeism and dry eye: the invisible loss on company payrolls

Dr. Philipe Saraiva Cruz · Ophthalmologist · CRM-MG 69.870 · RQE 71.903 · June 9, 2026 · 8 min read

When a company calculates the cost of a health problem affecting its team, it usually looks in the wrong place: sick notes. Absences are visible, countable, easy to add up. But in the case of dry eye, the sick note is the smallest part of the bill. The real loss lies in the people who keep working — and that is precisely why it goes unnoticed.

This phenomenon has a name: presenteeism. It is the loss of productivity of an employee who is physically present but whose performance is reduced by a health condition. Unlike absenteeism (being absent), presenteeism leaves no obvious trace on the time sheet — and, in low-severity chronic conditions such as dry eye, it tends to be the dominant cost component.

The math of the invisible loss

The economic logic is straightforward. Dry eye is rarely severe enough to justify an absence, but it is frequent and uncomfortable enough to degrade performance every day. Multiply a small daily loss by every day worked, by every affected employee, and the result is a substantial figure — only diluted and, therefore, invisible in traditional accounting.

Studies that measured the impact of dry eye on productivity found substantial losses attributable to presenteeism, with performance reductions that accumulate over the course of the workday[1]. When the weight of presenteeism is compared with that of absenteeism in this condition, the former largely dominates the cost equation.

~30%

drop in productivity from presenteeism has been documented in individuals with symptomatic dry eye — a loss that absenteeism alone would never capture.[1]

Why HR doesn't see it

There are three reasons why this cost slips past management radars:

  • It generates no sick note. The employee shows up, works the full shift and delivers the work — just more slowly and with more mistakes. No classic HR indicator records this.
  • It is attributed to something else. Slow reading becomes "lack of focus," rework becomes "carelessness," end-of-day fatigue becomes "overload." The ocular cause never enters the conversation.
  • The employee doesn't know it either. Because dry eye sets in gradually and fluctuates throughout the day, most people don't realize they are performing below their own capacity.

The combined effect is a continuous leak of productivity that no one attributes to its real source — and that, for this reason, is never treated.

With dry eye, the company doesn't lose days of work. It loses fractions of every day, from almost everyone, all the time.

Who is most exposed

The risk is concentrated exactly where the highest value-added work is found: teams that spend the day in front of screens. Software development, financial analysis, design, legal, customer service, administrative areas. As we saw in a previous article on visual display terminal workers, the prevalence of dry eye in this population can exceed 50%. In other words: presenteeism caused by dry eye is not a marginal problem of a few cases — it is a loss distributed across much of the workforce.

Prevention as a financial decision

The reversal of reasoning I propose to managers is this: treat digital eye health not as a "nice to have" benefit but as a productivity lever with measurable returns. First-line interventions are remarkably inexpensive:

  • A culture of structured breaks — the 20-20-20 rule (every 20 minutes, look at something 6 meters / 20 feet away for 20 seconds) costs just a few minutes per shift and preserves visual function throughout the day;
  • Visual ergonomics — monitors below eye level, glare control and adequate lighting;
  • Environment — humidification and reduction of direct air-conditioning flow over workstations;
  • Access to ophthalmic assessment — to identify and treat established cases before they become chronic.

In the face of a potential productivity loss in the tens of percentage points among those affected, the investment needed to mitigate the problem is disproportionately small. Few occupational health initiatives offer such a favorable cost-benefit ratio — precisely because the cause is simple, prevalent and largely preventable.

The real obstacle is execution

The challenge of structured breaks was never one of knowledge, but of adherence: in the middle of concentration, no one stops on their own.
