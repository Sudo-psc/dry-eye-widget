Dry eye in visual display terminal workers: the office's silent epidemic

Dr. Philipe Saraiva Cruz · Ophthalmologist · CRM-MG 69.870 · RQE 71.903 · June 9, 2026 · 7 min read

They don't walk into the clinic saying "I have dry eye." They walk in saying that the end of the day has become unbearable: burning, a gritty sensation, vision that blurs when reading the screen and clears when they blink hard. They are programmers, analysts, call center agents, designers, lawyers — anyone who spends hours in front of a video terminal. And, in most cases, the diagnosis is the same.

Dry eye disease has stopped being a complaint of the elderly to become one of the most prevalent occupational conditions of the digital age. A systematic review with meta-analysis dedicated specifically to video terminal workers (the so-called visual display terminals, or VDT) found prevalences that, in several studies, exceed 50% of this population[1]. In other words: in a room with ten people working at the computer, it is statistically likely that five have some degree of dry eye — many without even knowing it.

Why the screen dries out your eyes

The explanation is more mechanical than it might seem. At rest, we blink about 15 to 20 times per minute. This involuntary gesture spreads the tear film across the cornea like a windshield wiper — renewing the layer of tears that protects, lubricates, and keeps vision sharp.

When we focus on a screen, that reflex collapses. The blink rate drops dramatically, and — worse still — many of the remaining blinks are incomplete: the eyelid never fully closes. The result is that the tear film evaporates without being replenished. The ocular surface is left exposed, the tears lose stability, and the cycle of dryness, inflammation, and discomfort that characterizes the disease sets in.

Add to that the modern office environment, which conspires against the tears:

  • Air conditioning and low humidity accelerate evaporation of the tear film.
  • Monitors positioned above eye level increase the area of the ocular surface that is exposed — looking up opens the palpebral fissure wider.
  • Long, uninterrupted workdays leave no time for physiological recovery between tasks.
  • Contact lenses, common in this productive age group, worsen the condition.

Symptoms mistaken for "normal tiredness"

The big problem with occupational dry eye is that it disguises itself as routine. The signs are so gradual and so often attributed to a "tough day" that they rarely set off any alarm:

  • Burning or a stinging sensation late in the afternoon;
  • A foreign-body or gritty sensation in the eyes;
  • Vision that blurs intermittently and improves with blinking;
  • Red or heavy eyes at the end of the workday;
  • Paradoxically, excessive tearing — a defensive reflex against dryness;
  • Difficulty maintaining focus during prolonged reading.

Because these symptoms fluctuate and usually ease on weekends, many people live with them for years before seeking evaluation. It is the very definition of a silent epidemic.

What actually works

The good news is that the first line of prevention requires no medication and no expensive equipment — it requires a change in behavior in front of the screen. The most well-established measure is the 20-20-20 rule, recommended by international ophthalmology societies:

Every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away, for at least 20 seconds.

The mechanism is twofold. By shifting your gaze into the distance, the ciliary muscle relaxes and the convergence effort built up during near vision is released. And, during those few seconds of pause, complete blinking is naturally restored, redistributing the tear film across the cornea. It's simple, it's free, and it has solid physiological logic — the only challenge is remembering to do it, precisely when we are absorbed in work.

The strategy is rounded out by:

  • Positioning the monitor below eye level, to reduce the eyelid opening;
  • Humidifying the environment and avoiding direct airflow to the face;
  • Preservative-free artificial tears, when recommended by your ophthalmologist;
  • Conscious pauses to blink fully and deliberately;
  • An ophthalmological evaluation when symptoms persist — to distinguish evaporative dry eye, aqueous deficiency, and meibomian gland dysfunction, each with its own management.

From recommendation to practice

It was precisely the gap between knowing the 20-20-20 rule and following it that motivated the development of the Dry Eye Widget. Recommending the break in the clinic is easy; the hard part is making it actually happen in the middle of a spreadsheet or a line of code. A discreet reminder that follows the cycle silently and signals gently at the right moment turns the clinical recommendation into a sustainable habit — with no alarm, no aggressive interruption, and without collecting any data.

Occupational dry eye is real, measurable, and, in most cases, preventable. Treating it as "normal tiredness" is what keeps it silent. Recognizing it is the first step toward restoring comfort — and clarity — to millions of workdays.
