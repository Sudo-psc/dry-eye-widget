Remote work and dry eye: the home environment that dries out your eyes

Dr. Philipe Saraiva Cruz · Ophthalmologist · CRM-MG 69.870 · RQE 71.903 · June 9, 2026 · 7 min read

The shift to remote work solved many problems — and created others, quieter ones. Among them is a widespread decline in eye comfort that I hear about with growing frequency in my practice: "ever since I started working from home, my eyes have gotten worse." It is not just a perception. Remote work combines more screen time with environments that, in general, are worse for the tear film than the office.

Why working from home worsens dry eye

Dry eye disease is already highly prevalent among screen workers — reaching roughly half of that population in prevalence studies.[1] Remote work tends to push that risk even higher through a combination of factors:

  • Longer, more continuous screen sessions. Without the commute, without the walk to the meeting room, without the colleague interrupting from the next desk, uninterrupted time in front of the monitor increases — and with it, a sustained drop in blinking.
  • More video meetings. Video calls hold the gaze to the screen in an especially fixed way, reducing blink frequency even further.
  • Improvised ergonomics. A laptop on the kitchen table, a screen set too low or too high, an inadequate chair. Poorly positioned screens alter how wide the eyelids open and increase visual strain.
  • A dry home environment. Air conditioning, a fan pointed at the face, and low humidity accelerate evaporation of the tear film.
  • Blurred time boundaries. Without the ritual of "ending the workday," it is common to extend work late into the night, adding even more hours of exposure.

Building an eye-friendly workstation

The good news is that, at home, you have full control over the environment — you just need to know what to adjust:

Screen position

The top of the monitor should sit at eye level or slightly below, about an arm's length away. Looking slightly downward reduces the exposed ocular surface area and decreases evaporation. If you use a laptop, a stand with an external keyboard solves most of the problem.

Lighting and glare

Position your desk so the window is to the side, not behind or in front of the screen, to avoid glare and excessive contrast. Adjust the monitor's brightness so it is close to the ambient light level — screens much brighter or darker than their surroundings increase strain.

Air and humidity

Keep the airflow from the air conditioning or fan from blowing directly on your face. In very dry environments, a humidifier or even a container of water in the room helps preserve the tear film.

The habit that makes the biggest difference

No environmental adjustment replaces the single most important measure: breaking up continuous exposure with regular breaks. The 20-20-20 rule — every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds — is even more relevant at home, precisely because remote work removes the natural interruptions the office used to provide.

And here lies the trap: working alone and absorbed, it is easy to go two or three hours without looking away from the screen. The breaks that used to happen by chance — coffee with a colleague, an in-person meeting, a trip to the printer — have simply vanished. Without them, the break has to be deliberate.

The office interrupted us all the time. At home, no one interrupts — and the eyes foot the bill.

Turning the break into a sustainable habit

Artificially recreating those healthy interruptions was part of the motivation for developing the Dry Eye Widget. It works like that colleague who would stop by your desk and make you look up — only at the right clinical rhythm: a gentle, floating reminder that follows the 20-minute cycle and lets you know when it's time to look into the distance, without interrupting your work and without collecting any data. It is lightweight, starts with the system, and lives discreetly in the tray.

Remote work is here to stay, and with it the need to actively care for eye health — something the office's own rhythm used to do for us. With small environmental adjustments and the habit of taking breaks, it is perfectly possible to work from home without your eyes paying the price.
