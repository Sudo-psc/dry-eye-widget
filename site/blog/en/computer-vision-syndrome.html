Computer Vision Syndrome: the name for digital eye strain

Dr. Philipe Saraiva Cruz · Ophthalmologist · CRM-MG 69.870 · RQE 71.903 · June 9, 2026 · 7 min read

"Doctor, my eyes get tired, my vision blurs, I get a headache and my eyes feel dry — but only when I work at the computer. On the weekend it gets better." This description, repeated almost word for word by different patients, describes a clinical entity with a name of its own: Computer Vision Syndrome (CVS), also called digital eye strain.

CVS is not a single disease, but rather a cluster of symptoms — both ocular and visual — related to prolonged screen use. Dry eye is one of its central pieces, but not the only one. Understanding the full picture helps explain why so many people live with discomfort without ever receiving a clear diagnosis.

It's not one symptom, it's several

CVS usually brings together complaints of two kinds that overlap over the course of the day:

  • Related to the ocular surface: burning, a gritty sensation, red eyes, reflex tearing and the feeling of dry eye — all linked to reduced blinking in front of the screen.
  • Related to visual effort: blurred vision (intermittent or when shifting focus from near to far), difficulty refocusing, a frontal headache and pain around or behind the eyes.

There are also associated postural symptoms — neck and shoulder pain — because the way we position our head to see the screen directly influences ocular effort.

The three mechanisms behind the condition

Three physiological phenomena explain most of CVS:

1. Reduced blinking

Focused on the screen, we blink much less and incompletely. The tear film evaporates without being replenished, the ocular surface dries out and burning and blurring appear. This is the dry eye component of the syndrome — the same one that, among screen workers, affects about half of the population in prevalence studies.[1]

2. Accommodative effort

To see up close, the ciliary muscle inside the eye contracts continuously. Hours in this state produce accommodative fatigue — the sensation of "tired eyes" and the momentary difficulty in focusing on distant objects after a long time at the screen.

3. Convergence effort

The two eyes must converge to fixate on a near point. Held for hours, this sustained convergence fatigues the external eye muscles and contributes to the headache and periocular discomfort.

Why it improves on the weekend

The most useful diagnostic clue is the very fluctuation of the symptoms. CVS is exposure-dependent: it worsens over the course of the workday and eases when we reduce screen time. That is why the "it gets better on Saturday" is so characteristic — and that is also why the most effective intervention is precisely to break up continuous exposure with regular intervals.

What to do

Managing CVS combines habits, ergonomics and, when necessary, clinical treatment:

  • The 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something 6 meters (20 feet) away for 20 seconds. It acts on all three mechanisms at once — it restores blinking, relaxes accommodation and relieves convergence. It is the measure with the best cost-benefit ratio.
  • Screen ergonomics: monitor an arm's length away, with the top at eye level or slightly below, reducing how wide the eyelids open.
  • Control of reflections and glare: adjust the screen brightness to the surroundings and avoid reflections from windows and light fixtures.
  • Conscious blinking and, when indicated by the ophthalmologist, preservative-free artificial tears.
  • Adequate optical correction: uncorrected refractive errors greatly increase the effort — it is worth checking whether the glasses prescription is up to date for the screen distance.
Computer Vision Syndrome is not solved with an isolated eye drop, but by changing the way we interact with the screen throughout the day.

When to see an ophthalmologist

Symptoms that persist despite habit adjustments, constant blurred vision (and not just intermittent), significant eye pain or chronically dry eyes deserve evaluation. The examination makes it possible to distinguish the dry eye component — which may require specific treatment — from an uncorrected refractive problem or other causes.
